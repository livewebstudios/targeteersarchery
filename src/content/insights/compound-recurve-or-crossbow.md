People come in knowing they want a bow and not knowing which kind. Then they read forums for a
week and come back more confused, because every forum is full of people who are certain.

Here is the version we give across the counter.

## Recurve

A curved limb, a string, and nothing holding the weight for you. You pull the full draw weight
and you hold the full draw weight until you release.

**What it is good at.** Learning. There is nowhere to hide on a recurve: no let-off giving you a
rest, no sight pin doing your aiming, no release aid smoothing out a bad trigger finger. It builds
form faster than anything else, which is why almost every lesson we teach starts here and why
Olympic target archery is shot with one.

It is also the cheapest way in, the simplest to maintain, and the most portable.

**What it is not good at.** Holding. You cannot sit at full draw waiting for a deer to turn.
Practical hunting range is shorter and the margin for error is thinner.

**Who should buy one.** Anybody starting out. Anybody who wants the sport rather than the
equipment. Anybody drawn to traditional shooting.

## Compound

Cams at the ends of the limbs do the mechanical work. You pull through a peak weight, then the
cams roll over and the weight you are holding drops away. That is let-off, and on a modern bow
it can be eighty percent or more.

**What it is good at.** Everything, more or less. You can hold at full draw for a long moment.
Sights, a release aid and a stabilizer make it precise well past thirty yards. It is the standard
for bowhunting in New Jersey and for most target leagues.

**What it is not good at.** Being simple. A compound has more that can drift: cam timing, center
shot, peep rotation, string stretch. That is exactly why it wants a shop behind it, and why we
have a bench.

**Who should buy one.** Most hunters. Most league shooters. Anybody who has been through a few
lessons and wants a bow they will grow into for a decade.

## Crossbow

Horizontal limbs, a stock, a trigger, and a mechanism that holds the draw for you.

**What it is good at.** Removing draw weight from the equation entirely. Once it is cocked, it
stays cocked. That opens hunting up to people who cannot hold a compound at full draw: a bad
shoulder, an injury, age, a young hunter, anyone who is not going to build the strength for a
sixty-pound holding weight in one season. Scoped, it is precise at distance quickly.

**What it is not good at.** Being light or quiet. Cocking one takes a device and a bit of effort.
And the skill ceiling is genuinely lower, which some people like and some people find dull after
two seasons.

**Who should buy one.** Hunters who need it, hunters who want it, and anybody whose season is
otherwise going to be spent in a chair.

## The short answer

If you want to learn to shoot: recurve, in a lesson, with our equipment, before you spend money.

If you want to hunt and you can build the draw: compound.

If holding a draw is the barrier between you and the woods: crossbow, and do not let anybody make
you feel like that is a lesser choice.
