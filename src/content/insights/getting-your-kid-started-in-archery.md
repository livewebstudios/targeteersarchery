We put about a hundred and thirty kids through camp over a summer. Some of them come back for
league in the fall. Some of them come back fifteen years later with their own kids.

The parents' questions are always the same three, so here they are, answered.

## What age?

Seven. That is our rule for both the range and lessons, and it is not arbitrary.

At seven, most kids can hold a light bow steady enough to be safe, follow a range command
reliably, and remember a sequence of steps from one arrow to the next. Below that, the physical
part is usually fine and the attention part usually is not, and on a shooting line attention is
the safety system.

If your five-year-old is desperate, bring them in to watch a session. Sitting on the bench behind
the line for twenty minutes tells you a lot about whether they are ready, and it costs nothing.

## Do we need to buy anything?

No. Equipment is included in lessons and in camp. Please do not buy a bow first.

We say that a lot and people still turn up with a bow from a big-box store that is the wrong draw
length by four inches, because the box said "youth." A bow that does not fit teaches a child that
archery is hard and frustrating, and that lesson is very hard to undo.

Closed-toe shoes, hair tied back, and a shirt without loose sleeves. That is the whole list.

## What does the first session look like?

Shorter than you think, and closer to the target than you expect.

A child gets measured the same way an adult does: draw length, and a draw weight that is
genuinely light. Then safety, in words a seven-year-old can repeat back. Then shooting from a few
yards, where every arrow hits and the only thing to think about is the shot.

The instructor will spend most of the session on three things: a consistent stance, a consistent
anchor, and letting go without grabbing. That is it. Nothing about aiming, nothing about score.

Expect somewhere between ten and thirty arrows in a half hour. Kids tire faster than they admit,
and a tired archer builds bad habits, so a good instructor stops early.

## How do you know if it is going to stick?

Watch what happens after a miss.

Kids who are going to stay with this get curious after a bad arrow. They want to know why. Kids
who are not, get embarrassed, and that is worth noticing gently rather than pushing through.

The other tell is the walk to the target. A kid who runs down to pull arrows and comes back
talking is having a good time regardless of the score.

## Where it goes from there

Most kids follow the same path: a couple of lessons, then [summer camp](/kids/), then
[Friday Night Lights](/leagues/), our youth league. Two lines, eight weeks, starting at age
seven. League is where it turns social, and social is what makes it last.

Some of them get good enough that we start staying late for them. That has been happening in this
building since 1957.

## One thing to leave alone

Do not coach from the bench. We say this kindly and we say it every summer.

Your child has one voice to listen to on the line, and there is a reason it is not yours. Sit
back, watch, and let them tell you about it in the car. That is when you will get the real
version anyway.
